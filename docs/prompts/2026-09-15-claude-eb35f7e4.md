```text
Here's the chats feature as the PRD specifies it, pulled together from §4.5, §5.2, §6, §7, §8 and the Design phase spec.

## What it is

Independent conversations over the transcripts of channels you follow. A user has zero or more chats; each has an optional title and its own ordered messages (`docs/PRD.md:447`). Not per-channel — that's explicitly out of scope, along with chat deletion and archiving. Everything is retained.

## Retrieval scope

Every message searches **all channels the user currently follows that are approved** — paused or not (`docs/PRD.md:450`). There's no channel picker at chat creation and no chat-to-channel membership table; scope is computed per message from live eligibility (active follows ∩ approved, `docs/PRD.md:390`). Consequences are spelled out: following a new channel expands retrieval for existing chats, unfollowing or a decline excludes it from *future* retrieval, re-approval restores it — and history is never scrubbed. Old messages and their citations stay visible and can still serve as conversation context (`docs/PRD.md:452`).

## Answering a message

1. Embed the question.
2. Query Vectorize with `filter: { channelId: { $in: eligibleChannelIds } }`, `topK: 3`, all metadata, namespace `shared-catalog` (`docs/PRD.md:603`). Never an empty or unfiltered fallback query; if the channel list exceeds Vectorize's 2048-byte filter limit, split into several filters and merge by score rather than dropping the filter.
3. Validate each hit before use: episode still available, channel still eligible, and the vector's generation equals the episode's `active_vector_generation`. Fetch more candidates to replace rejects. Inactive or partial generations never enter retrieval (`docs/PRD.md:607`, `docs/PRD.md:850`).
4. Combine the chunks' exact text with the user's preferences and the chat's recent history, and ask Workers AI to answer with video/timestamp citations (`docs/PRD.md:458`).

## The no-follows case

Chat creation, message submission and history are **never disabled** for lack of follows. With no eligible channels, store the assistant reply verbatim — "Chat requires following at least one approved channel." — with no sources, and don't call AI or Vectorize at all (`docs/PRD.md:455`). The UI mirrors this: controls stay enabled, the fixed response does the explaining.

## Citations

Each reply stores a citation snapshot — episode and channel IDs, both titles, start time — so later catalog changes can't erase historical sources (`docs/PRD.md:461`). Rendered as up to three source cards, linking to `https://youtu.be/<episodeId>?t=<startSec>`. Assistant text renders as plain text with newlines preserved; only `youtube.com` URLs get linkified (`docs/PRD.md:624`).

## Storage (User DO, private)

Three tables plus preferences (`docs/PRD.md:498`):

- `chats` — `chat_id`, `title?`, `updated_at`
- `chat_messages` — `message_id`, `chat_id`, `sequence_number`, `role` (`user|assistant`), `content`, `status` (`pending|completed|failed`), `failure_code?`, `reply_to_message_id?`, `channel_id?`; unique `(chat_id, sequence_number)`
- `chat_message_sources` — `episode_id`, `channel_id`, `episode_title`, `channel_title`, `start_sec`; unique `(message_id, position)`
- `user_preferences` — singleton `id = 'default'`, `system_rules`

`chat_messages.channel_id` is reserved for a possible future scoped view and stays null today — it does *not* define retrieval scope. Content may be empty while a reply is pending. Chats, messages, preferences and read receipts never leave the User DO and are never logged (`docs/PRD.md:59`, `docs/PRD.md:817`).

## Routes (`docs/PRD.md:805`)

| Route | Notes |
|---|---|
| `POST /chats` / `GET /chats` | create empty chat / list own |
| `GET /chats/:id/messages?limit=50` | history with citation snapshots |
| `POST /chats/:id/messages` `{ message }` | reply + sources from current eligible follows |
| `GET` / `PUT /preferences` | `systemRules`, trimmed, ≤ 4000 chars, empty clears |

The preferences routes are already registered and tested — they just have no caller until M4 brings the Account field back. Explicitly not in scope: chat deletion, per-channel chats.

## Screens

`/chats` and `/chats/:id` — a rail of chats beside one transcript, roles in the left gutter, no bubbles, up to three source cards per reply (`docs/specs/design-phase.md:224`). Artboards exist for pending, failed, the fixed no-follows reply, an answer with a linkified URL, what unfollowing does to an old answer, the first run, and the phone. `Try again` resends the same question as a new attempt and keeps the failed reply above it. A chat is named by its first question — nothing generates a title.

**Three gaps M4 must close**, recorded in the design spec: no route sets `chats.title`, no route searches chats, and no chat can be deleted. Also note the nav decision of 2026-09-15 (`docs/PRD.md:1409`) — there is currently no Chats nav item, no `/chats` route (it falls through to `/queue`), and no chat-rules field on Account; the placeholder and its `Unbuilt` component were deleted rather than stubbed, so M4 builds from the design.
```

```text
First, a framing point: **"global by default" and "no targeting affordance at all" are two separate decisions bundled into one.** The first is clearly right. The second is the one worth re-examining, and the schema already leaves a door open for it (`chat_messages.channel_id`, nullable, "retained for a possible future scoped view", `docs/PRD.md:506`).

## Pros

**The question you can't route is the question worth asking.** Targeting requires the user to know where the answer lives before they ask. But if you knew which episode covered it, you'd open that episode's summary — the product already has `/read/:episodeId`. Untargeted chat is aimed squarely at the case the rest of the product doesn't serve: "somebody said something about X, I don't remember who."

**Cross-source synthesis is the only thing here a transcript search can't do.** "How do different hosts frame X" is answerable only with a global pool. Scope it to one episode and chat degrades into a slightly better Ctrl-F — and the product's whole premise is a digest *across* followed channels.

**One scope concept, learned once.** Follow governs digest, queue, and chat alike (`docs/PRD.md:390`). A picker is a second control that can contradict the first, and then you owe the user an answer for every disagreement: I'm scoped to a channel I just unfollowed — now what?

**No scope state means no scope bugs.** The PRD's invariant — retrieval narrows, history is never scrubbed (`docs/PRD.md:452`) — has exactly one answer for unfollow, decline, and re-approve. Scoped chats would need a policy for each, *per chat*, and a UI to explain it.

**The sequencing is right even if targeting eventually wins.** Global → targeted is cheap: scope is computed per message, so adding a hint needs no migration. Targeted → global means reconciling chats that already carry scope. Ship global first regardless of where you land.

## Cons

**Precision degrades exactly as the product succeeds.** `topK: 3` over an unguided pool, and the design explicitly targets 30 follows (`docs/PRD.md:926`). A question whose words appear in many transcripts returns three chunks that may be from three unrelated shows, and nothing in the query expresses which one you meant.

**Widest search, narrowest read.** Untargeted retrieval needs *more* candidates to compensate for a noisier pool, but the answer budget is fixed at three and capped upward by the 24k-token model context. Targeting would spend the same three slots on a pool where three is plenty.

**No repair path when retrieval misses.** `Try again` resends the same question (`docs/specs/design-phase.md:229`), so it doesn't help. The only correction available to the user is rephrasing with more distinguishing words — query engineering, pushed onto the reader.

**A seam between reading and asking.** The natural moment to ask is mid-episode on `/read/:episodeId`. At that moment the product's answer is "go to `/chats` and describe the episode in prose." That's the sharpest edge in the current design.

**Silence is ambiguous.** Three source cards are the user's only evidence of what was searched. A thin answer can't be distinguished from "nothing was said about this" versus "the retriever missed it." Silent misses cost more trust than visible wrong answers.

**Multi-voice attribution is a measured weakness in this stack.** `docs/specs/summary-quality.md:102` records three A/Bs: `llama-3.3-70b` has a stylistic prior for "The guest…" that prompting does not shift — a participant-extraction call put "David Freeberg (guest, former White House advisor)" directly in the prompt and still produced **0% named**, plus a possibly-hallucinated name. Untargeted retrieval maximizes distinct voices per prompt, which is precisely the worst case for a fault already tried and abandoned. The source cards do carry channel and episode titles, so citation-level attribution survives; what's at risk is in-prose conflation across three shows, with the reader left to map card back to sentence.

**Filter-splitting isn't free.** The 2048-byte limit forces splitting channel lists and merging by score (`docs/PRD.md:604`). Scores from different filter subsets aren't one ranking in practice. A scoped query mostly avoids that path.

## What I'd do

Keep global as the default and don't build a picker. Add one thing instead: **an "ask about this" entry point on the reading screen** that passes the episode as a per-message scope hint — not a scoped chat object, not a membership table. That is deliberately *not* the per-channel chat the PRD lists as a non-goal (`docs/PRD.md:46`); chats stay global, and only the individual message carries a hint. It closes the reading→asking seam, which is the con I'd rank highest, at roughly the cost of one parameter.

Then instrument M4 to answer the question you can't answer today: do the three citations for a given question cluster in one episode, or spread? That's free to collect from `chat_message_sources` and it's the evidence that should decide whether a real picker ever gets built.
```

```text
Concretely, it's one optional field on one request — no new screens, no picker, no stored scope.

## What the reader sees

On `/read/:episodeId`, next to the summary, a control: **Ask about this episode**. It opens a chat with the question box focused and a small removable chip above it — `About: <episode title> ✕`. You type, you send. The chip applies to **that one message** and then clears; the next message in the same chat is global again unless you attach another.

That's the entire surface. The only way to set scope is to arrive from something you were reading — scope is a byproduct of where you came from, never a form you fill in.

## What changes underneath

**Request.** `POST /chats/:id/messages` goes from `{ message }` to `{ message, aboutEpisodeId? }`.

**Retrieval.** Today it's `filter: { channelId: { $in: eligibleChannelIds } }`, `topK: 3` (`docs/PRD.md:603`). With a hint present, filter to that episode instead. I'd start with hard scope — only that episode — because it's simple and the chip says plainly what it did. Blending (episode's best two, then backfill globally) is the obvious v2 if the data says people ask comparative questions.

**The invariant that matters: the hint narrows, never widens.** You still confirm the episode's channel is in the caller's eligible set before using it. Otherwise the parameter becomes a way to read chunks from a channel you don't follow or one that's been declined — eligibility is the gate, and the hint is a filter applied inside it.

**Storage.** A new nullable `about_episode_id` on `chat_messages`. One correction to what I said earlier: `chat_messages.channel_id` is channel-grained, so it can't carry an episode hint — what the PRD anticipated (`docs/PRD.md:506`) is that per-message scope might exist at all, not this specific column.

## What it deliberately is not

- **Not a picker.** No channel list, no checkboxes, nothing to configure.
- **Not per-channel chat** — the PRD non-goal at `docs/PRD.md:46`. No chat is bound to a channel, `chats` gains no column, no membership table. Two consecutive messages in one chat can carry different hints or none.
- **Not persistent.** The hint doesn't survive the message. Nothing goes stale when you unfollow, because no chat stores scope. A past message keeps its hint as a record of what happened, the same way its citation snapshots do.

That last point is the whole reason for the design. Every expensive problem with scoped chats — scope drifting when you unfollow, two controls that can disagree, a reconciliation policy per chat — comes from scope living **on the chat**. Per-message scope has none of it.

## Cost

Smaller than it sounds, and I checked:

- The `episodeId` metadata index **already exists** on the Vectorize index — the config test guards it at `apps/api/test/wrangler-config.test.ts:175`. No index rebuild, which would otherwise be the blocker (vectors written before an index exists aren't filterable on that field).
- The helper's filter type is the narrow part: `apps/api/src/lib/vectorize.ts:39` types it as `{ channelId: { $in: string[] } }` only. Widening that type, plus the test fake that filters on `channelId` alone (`apps/api/src/lib/vectorize.ts:299`), is the real work.
- Then one nullable column, one optional request field, one button, one chip.

## Open calls worth deciding before building

Three, all genuine:

1. **Does the chip stick across follow-ups?** I've described it clearing after one message. Sticky-until-dismissed is more convenient and less predictable — a reader may not notice it's still on three questions later. Worth testing rather than assuming.
2. **What if the episode isn't summarised yet?** Simplest is that the control only appears on an episode with a published summary and an active vector generation.
3. **What if the channel goes ineligible between opening the chat and sending?** The reply should say so rather than silently answering globally — silent scope changes are the trust problem I flagged.
```

```text
## URL shape

Query param — `/chats/:id?about=<episodeId>` — not a path segment.

The rule that decides it: **a path identifies a resource, a query modifies a representation of it.** There is no "about view of chat X" entity; it's the same chat with a filter applied. A path segment would also force two route patterns for one screen (scoped and not) and produce a URL that looks broken when scope is off. Strip a query param and you still have a valid chat URL — that's the property you want when a chip is dismissable.

Worth separating: **this is a web-route decision, not an API one.** The API keeps scope in the request body — `POST /chats/:id/messages { message, aboutEpisodeId? }` — because scope is a property of the message being created, not of the collection being addressed. Two different layers, two different idioms, and they shouldn't be made to match.

## "No global start" — what it buys and what it costs

This is a bigger move than the other two, and I think it's mostly right.

It buys the thing I'd worried about most. My sharpest con was precision collapsing over an unguided pool at `topK: 3`; if every chat is *born* scoped, the first answer in every conversation is well-retrieved. It also kills the blank-box cold start, where a user meets an empty input, tests it with the question it's worst at ("summarize everything"), gets a mediocre answer, and never returns. And strategically it makes chat a continuation of reading rather than a destination competing with it — which retroactively makes the 2026-09-15 nav decision (`docs/PRD.md:1409`) permanent rather than temporary: nav stays Queue and Sources, and `/chats` becomes a history of conversations rather than a front door.

Two concrete spec conflicts it creates, both worth deciding before M4 writes a line:

- `POST /chats` currently creates an **empty** chat (`docs/PRD.md:805`). If chats can only originate from an episode, that route either retires or always carries an origin.
- The fixed no-follows reply — "Chat requires following at least one approved channel." (`docs/PRD.md:455`) — becomes close to unreachable, since you can't land on a reading screen with no follows. That's spec'd behavior with tests behind it that this decision may quietly retire.

And one fork you haven't settled: **can you dismiss the chip and thereby widen an existing chat to global?** "No way to *start* a global chat" isn't the same as "no way to *reach* one." I'd keep dismissal. The goal is that every chat is born in context, not that cross-source questions are forbidden — and cross-source synthesis is the one thing chat does that a transcript search can't. Removing dismissal would cut the feature's ceiling to buy very little.

## Is it actually useful?

The bar isn't "is a chat nice." It's **what does this add over the summary already on the screen**, which is structured, sectioned, and has takeaways. If chat mostly restates the summary, it costs inference and adds a surface for nothing.

Three places it genuinely clears that bar:

1. **Detail below the summary's resolution.** Summaries compress an hour into sections and takeaways. The specific number, the company named once, the caveat the host attached to the claim — that's in the transcript and nowhere else. Chat is the only path to it. This is the strongest case, and episode-scoped retrieval is exactly good at it.
2. **"Did they actually say that?"** Verification of a half-remembered claim, or one you saw quoted elsewhere. Timestamped citations make it checkable in one click.
3. **Cross-episode synthesis.** The differentiated use, now one dismissal away rather than the default.

Three where it's weak, and one of them is measured in this repo:

- Anything needing more than three chunks — roughly three minutes of speech. "Summarize the episode" is already done better upstream.
- General-knowledge drift: without care the model answers from its weights rather than the retrieved text, and the citations make that failure look authoritative.
- **Cross-source attribution.** `docs/specs/summary-quality.md:102` records three A/Bs: `llama-3.3-70b` has a stylistic prior for "The guest…" that prompting does not shift — even with "David Freeberg (guest, former White House advisor)" in the prompt it produced **0% named**. Untargeted retrieval maximizes distinct voices per prompt, which is the worst case for a fault already tried and abandoned.

**Verdict: this is a depth tool, not a discovery tool.** Global-first would have made it a mediocre discovery tool. Originating from reading pushes it toward its strength. I'd hold that line in the design — and I'd resist any later pressure to add a global "ask anything" box on Queue, because that recreates the blank box you just eliminated.

One measurement warning: chat will be **low-frequency, and that's not failure.** Judged on daily usage it looks dead. The right question is what share of *read* episodes generate a question, and whether readers who ask return more.

## ICP

Caveat first, because it changes the exercise: the PRD says Said on Air is "a personal, long-lived tool for a small, trusted set of users" (`docs/PRD.md:24`). So ICP here isn't market sizing — it's **the use pattern you're optimizing for**, and making it explicit stops you building for an imagined audience. The owner is user zero, and the honest test is whether the owner *is* the ICP.

**Primary: the analyst-listener.** Follows 15–30 shows in a domain where claims have consequences — investing, policy, an industry they trade or build in. Listens for specifics: numbers, names, positions, predictions. The defining trait isn't volume, it's that **they act on what was said and being wrong is costly.** That's what makes a timestamped citation worth more to them than a good summary. A casual listener doesn't care if a summary is slightly off; this person does.

**Sharpest wedge: the writer or researcher** — newsletter, memo, deck, podcast of their own. Retrieval-with-citation *is* their workflow, not an enhancement to it. Smallest segment, highest attachment, and the best source of feature feedback because they'll notice a bad citation immediately.

**Not the ICP, despite looking like it:** the keeping-up professional following fifteen AI podcasts. High volume, but their need is skimming, and the summary already serves it. They'll try chat twice. Also not: the casual listener with three shows, who won't ask anything.

Behavioral signature to look for: follows well past ten channels, the same few shows recur in their questions, questions are specific rather than "summarize this," and — the strongest tell — **they click the source link.**
```

```text
## How we planned to use it

`answer()` returns the text plus a truncated flag; `lib/chat.ts` sees the flag and calls `failAssistantMessage(messageId, "ANSWER_TRUNCATED")`. The reply row goes to `status: failed` and the UI renders the failed state with `Try again`.

That's a bad trade, and it's what my proposal actually did.

## Why it matters — with one correction to my own framing

I called it a "silent failure." That's wrong from the reader's side. A sentence ending *"...and rewriting of the"* is glaringly visible — the probe output proves it. It's silent to the **system**, not to the person.

So the real UX harms are different from what I implied:

- **It's a reproducible dead end.** `Try again` resends the same question (`design-phase.md` §4.9). Same prompt, same chunks, same cap — it very likely truncates in the same place. The only recovery the product offers is one that doesn't work.
- **The reader can't attribute the fault.** Is the model bad, is the transcript thin, or did something break? Nothing on screen distinguishes them, so the reasonable inference is "this product is broken."
- **It contradicts the core promise.** Said on Air is a verification tool. Visibly broken output is disproportionately damaging to something whose whole claim is carefulness.

## What I'd do instead

**Trim to the last complete sentence, keep the answer and its sources, store it `completed`, and disclose it in one line.**

Concretely: on `finish_reason === "length"`, cut the dangling fragment at the final sentence boundary, store what remains with its citations, and mark the message truncated. The UI shows a quiet line under the answer — *"Answer shortened."* — and nothing else changes.

Three reasons this beats an error:

1. **The answer is usually in the first paragraph.** Retrieval-grounded replies front-load: the direct answer, then elaboration. Truncation eats elaboration. Discarding the whole reply to protect the reader from a missing tail inverts the value.
2. **The dangling fragment is the actual damage**, and trimming removes it for free. What made the probe output look broken wasn't missing content — it was the half-sentence.
3. **The recovery already exists and it's the product's own mechanism.** This is a *chat*. If the reader wants more, they ask. That's better than a `Continue` button, which would need a continuation prompt — and continuation seams from this model class are poor — and better than `Try again`, which reproduces the failure.

## What I'd reject

**Auto-continue** (detect, call again, concatenate): doubles latency on a call the reader is sitting through, doubles neuron cost, needs a loop bound, and produces visible seams. Wrong trade for an edge case.

**Instructing brevity in the prompt**: this is the model that ignored an explicit ban on "the speaker" across three A/Bs (`summary-quality.md` §4.3). Length instructions are not something to rely on here.

**Doing nothing**: the fragment stays, and we learn nothing.
```

```text
## What the reader actually experiences

A scoped question comes from `Ask` on a summary — the reader is already *inside* that episode. They've read the summary; they're asking because it didn't have the detail they wanted. ## Why that's worse than it sounds

**It silently misrepresents the evidence.** One card implies one passage. The reader reasonably infers the whole answer came from 52:07 — so if they click through and the claim isn't there, the product looks like it hallucinated. It didn't; the supporting passage was at 12:03 and we hid it. That's a trust failure manufactured entirely by presentation.

**It breaks the verification loop, which is the ICP's entire job.** The analyst-listener acts on what was said and being wrong is costly; source click-through is the health metric precisely because verification is the value. A scoped answer hands them one verifiable claim out of eight.

**It inverts the asymmetry we deliberately paid for.** We set `SCOPED_KEEP = 8` *above* `UNSCOPED_KEEP = 6` on the argument that one episode is one voice, so depth is free. ## The actual insight

Dedupe isn't wrong. It's applied at the wrong **granularity for one of the two modes**:

- **Unscoped, the unit of citation is the episode.** Six chunks from six shows; "how many shows covered this" is the legible thing. Collapsing three chunks of one show into one card is correct.
- **Scoped, the unit of citation is the moment.** One episode is a given. The only information a citation can carry is *when*.

Same word — "source" — two different meanings, because the reader's question is different.

Don't dedupe in the API at all. **Store every validated chunk as a source, and group by episode at render time.**

`chat_message_sources` already permits this — its unique constraint is `(message_id, position)`, not `(message_id, episode_id)`. Multiple rows for one episode are legal today. Dedupe is doing *presentation* work in the storage layer, which is why it produces the wrong result in one mode.

With grouping in the reader-facing layer, one rule serves both:

- Scoped → one card, several timestamps: *"I did it 8 Times! · 12:03 · 34:40 · 52:07 · 1:18:22"*
- Unscoped → one card per episode, usually one timestamp each

This is strictly better than the option I proposed last message. ## What drift does to a reader
```

```text
The user-visible artifact of a stale metadata index is **a confident false negative**.

Not an error page. Not a spinner that never resolves. A complete, well-formed, entirely plausible sentence: *"Nothing in what you follow covers that."* — about an episode they follow, that was ingested, summarised, chunked and vectorized, and that demonstrably does cover it.

Every layer reported success. Vectorize answered a filter on an unindexed property with zero matches and no error. Retrieval ran. Validation ran. Outcome 4 fired, which is a branch we deliberately built and tested. The reply was stored `completed`. Nothing anywhere was wrong except the answer.

## Why that's the worst available failure mode here

**It's indistinguishable from the truth.** A true "nothing covers that" and a broken retrieval produce the *identical sentence*. A crash would be better — a crash says "something is wrong with us." This says "you're wrong about what's in there."

**It teaches abandonment, quietly.** A reader asks two or three scoped questions, gets that sentence each time, and concludes the feature doesn't work or their episodes aren't searchable. They stop using it. And they **never file a bug**, because from their side nothing broke — the product just wasn't useful. Silent failures don't produce bug reports; they produce churn you can't attribute.

**It attacks the specific thing this product sells.** Said on Air is a verification tool; its claim is *what was said*. A false negative is the product asserting something *wasn't* said when it was. That's worse than a wrong answer with a citation — a citation can be checked. A negative offers the reader nothing to check.
```
