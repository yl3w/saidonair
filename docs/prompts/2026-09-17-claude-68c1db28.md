Here's the whole thing, concretely.

## What a "generation" is

Every vector id is `episodeId:generationId:chunkIndex`. The generation is a fresh UUID minted each time an episode is ingested. It exists because re-ingesting an episode — a better transcript, a new prompt — must not leave the index half-old and half-new while the work is in flight. So a re-ingest writes a **complete second set** of vectors under a new generation id, and only when all of them are safely in does the Registry flip `active_vector_generation` to the new one and delete the old set.

Your episode `pduZ-bfcKAQ` ("I did it 8 Times!", 105 chunks) went through exactly that:

```
2026-09-15 17:27  publish  → generation 04109673…   (105-ish vectors written)
2026-09-16 21:42  replace  → generation f3409a13…   (105 vectors written, made active)
                            → and 04109673…'s vectors should have been deleted here
```

They weren't. Both sets are still sitting in `media-rag-dev`. The Registry is perfectly consistent — it says `active = f3409a13…` and that's true. The index just has 105 extra vectors nobody will ever be allowed to use.

## Why this causes no wrong answers

Retrieval reads the generation back out of each returned id and throws away anything that doesn't match the episode's active generation (`chat.ts:validate`). So a dead vector can never be quoted, never be cited, never reach the model. **Nothing a reader sees is wrong.** That's why this is not the Steve Jobs bug — that one put a wrong card in front of you; this one is invisible.

## Why it costs you anyway

The rejection happens *after* Vectorize has already chosen what to send. Vectorize can't filter on generation — it's buried inside the id string, and there's no metadata index on it (PRD §6 says so explicitly). So:

- Chat asks for the **top 24** chunks.
- Vectorize returns 24, ranked by similarity, some of which are dead.
- Validation drops the dead ones.
- You're left acting on fewer than 24 — and the real chunks that *would* have been 23rd and 24th were never sent.

Measured on your index:

| | asked for | usable |
|---|---|---|
| Sweep of the Knowledge Project channel | 50 | 33 — **17 slots were the dead generation** |
| Live "quantum computing" question, yesterday's walkthrough | 24 | 22 |

For that one episode, the dead set was taking a third of the candidate window in its own channel. It's silent shrinkage of the pool you draw from.

## And it matters more since yesterday than it did before

Before the reranker, depth barely mattered — the code took the top 6 by cosine and that was that. The whole reason the Eilish Poe fix works is that the *best* chunk was sitting at candidate **7 of 24** and the reranker pulled it up to first. That only works if the window is genuinely 24 wide. Every dead vector in it is one fewer real candidate the reranker gets to consider. The two features interact: the leak was harmless when nothing looked past rank 6, and it isn't now.

## Why nobody noticed

`discard()` in `ingest.ts:689` is written to swallow its own failures — deliberately, and the doc comment says so: *"a failure is logged and never changes the attempt's outcome."* That's the right call for the attempt (a published episode shouldn't be marked failed because a cleanup blipped), but it means:

- a cleanup that worked and a cleanup that didn't look identical afterwards,
- nothing retries it,
- nothing ever counts vectors against `chunk_count` to notice the mismatch,
- and there's no user-visible symptom to complain about.

The log line that would have said why is long gone from dev output.

## How widespread — probably one episode, not a pattern

Five episodes have been replaced (all five Knowledge Project ones). In the candidate samples I took, only `pduZ-bfcKAQ` showed two generations; the other four showed exactly one each. So this reads as **one cleanup that failed once**, not as cleanup being broken — which is consistent with the code being correct and having no way to notice a transient failure.

That's a sample, though, not a census. The precise check is cheap and I haven't run it: for each replaced episode, ask Vectorize `getByIds` for the *old* generation's ids (both generation ids are recorded in `episode_ingestion_attempts`) and see which come back. That would say exactly how many strays exist — and, because a re-run of `discard` needs `getByIds` to see them, it also tells us whether the fix is simply "run the cleanup again" or something more.

Want me to run that census, and then fix it? The fix itself has two halves worth separating: sweeping the strays that already exist, and giving the product a way to *notice* next time — a vector count against `chunk_count`, since a silent failure that nothing detects will happen again.

## As a PM: the choice isn't *whether* to delete, it's *when* — and the harm is wildly asymmetric

Two ways to get this wrong, and they are not comparable.

**Delete late (today's bug).** The candidate window shrinks. I measured it: 17 of 50 slots in that channel, 2 of 24 on a real question. No user ever sees it. Cost is a slow quality tax you'd never get a bug report about.

**Delete early.** You delete vectors the product is actively serving. Picture the window: an episode is `available`, its summary is on screen, a reader hits Ask — and its vectors were dropped minutes ago while the replacement is still embedding. Retrieval returns nothing. And because of the decision you made yesterday, the reply is not a weak answer, it's:

> Nothing in this episode covers that.

Confident, clean, and **false** — about the episode they are looking at. That's the single worst failure this product can produce, because the reader has no way to tell it from the truth. Yesterday's floor-both call was right, and it *raises* the cost of a delete-early bug: it converted a mushy answer into an authoritative refusal.

This asymmetry is the whole design constraint. Publish-then-delete isn't an implementation preference, it's the thing standing between you and the only failure mode that lies to a reader. **Never invert it.** If "each time an episode is summarized, get rid of the old generation" means *at the start of re-summarization*, that's the trap — the new generation takes minutes to build, and that's minutes of a live episode answering nothing.

## As a UX designer: the reader has no mental model of a "generation," and must never acquire one

The reader's model is flat: *this episode is here, and I can ask about it.* Generations, staging, vector counts — none of it exists for them, and none of it should ever surface. No "re-indexing…" state, no "this episode is being updated" banner, no disabled Ask button. Every one of those trades an invisible engineering problem for a visible user problem.

Which gives you a crisp design requirement, and it's stronger than "clean up eventually":

> **A replace must be atomic from the reader's point of view.** At no instant may an episode be summarized-but-unsearchable.

Everything else — when you delete, how you retry, whether you sweep — is free to vary as long as that holds. And notice it's also the cheapest possible UX: doing this right means the reader never learns the concept exists.

## What your proposal adds that today's code lacks

The valuable part of "each time an episode is summarized" is not the delete — it's the **repetition**. Today's cleanup fires once and, if it misses, never fires again. Make it run on every ingestion pass and it stops being an attempt and becomes a *retry*: the system heals itself on the path that naturally recurs.

Safely stated, it's two calls, not one:

- **At attempt start** — delete every generation for this episode that is neither the active one nor the one about to be staged. Safe by construction: what's serving is excluded by name. This is where the pduZ-bfcKAQ stray would finally die.
- **At publish** — delete the generation that just stopped being active. Exactly today's behaviour, kept.

Both idempotent, both reconstructable from the ledger, and a failure at either point is simply picked up next time.

**The honest limitation:** an episode never re-ingested stays dirty forever. For fifteen episodes and a personal tool, I'd accept that rather than build a sweep cron — but it's a real gap, so it should be a known one rather than an assumed-away one.

## The thing I'd push back on

Self-healing fixes the strays but still leaves you blind. `discard()` swallowing its failures is correct for the *attempt* (a published episode shouldn't be marked failed over a cleanup blip) — but it means a cleanup that worked and one that didn't are indistinguishable afterwards, and a log line in dev evaporates.

Since the ledger already computes the orphan set, detection is nearly free: comparing recorded generations against the active one is a query, and it can run wherever you'd notice it. **A silent failure with no observable will happen again**, and next time there may be no Steve Jobs card to make you go looking.
